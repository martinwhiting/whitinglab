The species behind the science

Study animals

01 / 13

Social skinks

Black rock skinks (Egernia saxatilis) and tree skinks (E. striolata) feature heavily in our work on social development, family living and cognition.

02 / 13

Augrabies flat lizards

Platysaurus broadleyi from Augrabies Falls National Park, South Africa—stars of numerous BBC documentaries and Martin's favourite!

03 / 13

African flat lizards

Our long-standing work on the taxonomy and systematics of Platysaurus includes Attenborough’s flat lizard, described in honour of Sir David Attenborough.

04 / 13

Blue-tongue and desert skinks

Our study species include the centralian blue-tongued skink, great desert skink, White’s skink and the sleepy lizard, or shingleback.

05 / 13

Toad-headed agamas

With Dr Yin Qi at the Chengdu Institute of Biology, we study signalling in Chinese Phrynocephalus, including P. mystaceus and P. vlangalii.

06 / 13

More Phrynocephalus

Toad-headed agamas from China are famous for their complex dynamic visual signals (tail waves).

07 / 13

Jackson’s chameleons

We compared colour signals in introduced Hawaiian and native Kenyan populations of Trioceros jacksonii, exploring how signals evolve when major predators are absent.

08 / 13

Australian reptiles

Our work also includes water dragons, eastern water skinks, Jacky dragons and small-eyed snakes.

09 / 13

Asian lizards

We have worked with Ruchira Somaweera on the leaf-nosed lizard (Ceratophora tennentii) in Sri Lanka and the crocodile lizard (Shinisaurus crocodilurus) in China.

10 / 13

Frogs and toads

Our amphibian research includes cane toads, whirring tree frogs, African foam-nest frogs and spotted grass frogs.

11 / 13

Australian skink families

Three-toed, gidgee, Cunningham’s, White’s and black rock skinks—along with sleepy lizards—help us investigate the evolution of family living.

12 / 13

Magnificent tree frog

Litoria splendida, Australia’s largest tree frog, has been studied by lab members in the Kimberley and in our animal facilities.

13 / 13

Perentie

A camera trap captured this Varanus giganteus at Curtin Springs in the Northern Territory during field work by Kari Soennichsen.
